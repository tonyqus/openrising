# Choose your playing field: geographic arbitrage for your first opportunity

[Full guide](geographic-arbitrage.md) · [Budget assumptions and source notes](geographic-arbitrage-budget.md)

Prepared: 2026-11-16.

Finding work also means finding a place that needs what you can credibly offer.

Geographic arbitrage uses differences in earning opportunities, living costs and demand for skills to improve your options. It can mean moving to a different labour market, working remotely from another location, or choosing a more affordable neighbourhood within commuting distance.

Digital nomadism separates work from a fixed office. Geographic arbitrage asks how a location choice changes opportunities and disposable income. Neither requires constant travel.

## Compare opportunities before moving

| Dimension | Evidence to collect |
| --- | --- |
| Demand for your skills | Recent, relevant employer vacancies |
| Access for beginners | Genuine junior roles, internships and training |
| Eligibility | Employer location rules, language and work eligibility |
| Monthly surplus | Obtainable take-home pay minus a complete personal budget |
| Growth | Mentoring, useful projects and transferable skills |

A low unemployment rate does not establish that an international graduate can obtain a particular job. National statistics are not city statistics; missing data must remain missing. Read the [labour-market comparison](city-youth-unemployment.md) with its definitions.

## Calculate a complete budget

Monthly surplus = take-home income minus rent, food, transport, utilities, communications, necessary healthcare or insurance, other expenses and monthly allocations for annual costs.

Calculate startup cash separately, including deposits and moving costs. Compare central housing, non-central housing and shared accommodation. Calculate in local currency first and record the date and direction of any exchange rate.

The earlier Shanghai–London discussion included a possible surplus of roughly CNY9,000 per month in London. The [budget companion](geographic-arbitrage-budget.md) preserves the historical figures, flags missing source snapshots and reconstructs an explicitly hypothetical scenario. It is not a current salary survey or a promise of savings.

## Learn from charts without overstating them

The [reference analysis project](https://github.com/Aventardo7777/digital-nomad-data-analysis) describes a course assignment with simulated collection. Its charts can teach comparison and visualisation; they do not establish population-wide facts.

- Challenge charts prompt questions about practical constraints.
- Income and satisfaction scatterplots show patterns, not causation.
- City radar charts depend on chosen criteria and weights.
- Occupation heatmaps suggest questions about skill demand, not guaranteed salaries.
- City cost scatterplots invite comparison, not a universal relocation ranking.

## Run a small experiment

Choose one occupation and three cities. Collect about 20 recent, deduplicated relevant roles per city, identify those you can actually apply for, obtain multiple housing quotes and build baseline and adverse budgets. Apply while researching. Use employer responses and written conditions to validate the opportunity before relocating.

Publish the small analysis as a reproducible portfolio contribution, with sources, dates, assumptions and limitations. Contribute a modest improvement to an existing project where possible. Identify AI assistance honestly and never invent experience, data or sources.
